A delayed penalty is an NHL referee's signal that an infraction has occurred but play can continue temporarily. It is not a promise that the offending player will escape punishment. The distinction matters most when the non-offending team scores before the whistle: depending on the penalties involved, a goal can cancel a pending minor penalty without erasing every possible penalty.

## Why the whistle does not sound immediately

Under NHL Rule 15, when the team that did not commit the infraction controls the puck, the referee can signal the penalty and allow play to continue. The official raises an arm. Play normally stops when the offending team gains possession and control. Merely touching or deflecting the puck does not necessarily amount to control. This is why a defender can brush a pass and the attacking team may still keep playing under the raised arm.

Imagine Blue commits a hooking infraction while Red has the puck. Red continues cycling in the offensive zone, and the referee keeps an arm raised. If Blue establishes control, the whistle sounds and the hooking penalty is assessed. If Red keeps control and shoots, the shot is part of the same continuing play. The referee is not granting Red a new power play yet; the penalty is waiting to be enforced at the stoppage.

The difference between possession and a deflection is crucial for spectators. A puck that ricochets off a defending skate may remain live, while a defender who collects the puck and can make a play normally triggers the whistle. The official's judgment about control determines the precise moment of the stoppage.

## Why teams pull their goalie during the delay

The non-offending team often replaces its goalie with an extra skater during a delayed penalty. Because the whistle will normally come when the offending team gains control, the attacking team can add a sixth skater and create a temporary numerical advantage. This is an attacking choice, not a requirement of Rule 15. It is also not risk-free: a mishandled pass can enter the attacking team's own unguarded net while the delayed call remains active.

For example, Red has six skaters and no goalie while Blue awaits a minor penalty. Red's defenseman passes backward, the puck misses the intended teammate and travels into Red's empty goal. A goal against Red is possible even though Blue has not established possession. The delayed-penalty signal does not automatically invalidate a goal scored through the non-offending team's own action. Teams therefore use the extra attacker with care, especially near the blue line.

This substitution differs from pulling the goalie late in a game. During a delayed call, the attacking team expects a stoppage once the other side controls the puck. Late in a game, the team may be exposed to an empty-net shot as soon as the opponent wins the puck. Both tactics create an extra attacker, but the circumstances and immediate incentives are different.

## What a goal does to a pending penalty

NHL Rule 15.2 addresses the common case: if the non-offending team scores while a delayed minor penalty is pending, the minor ordinarily is not imposed. The goal is the immediate benefit, so the same ordinary minor is not added afterward. That is not a blanket rule for every infraction. Major penalties and other penalties that cannot be canceled by a goal remain subject to their own rules.

Consider a straightforward example. Blue is about to receive a two-minute tripping minor. Red maintains control and scores before Blue touches the puck with control. The goal counts, and the pending ordinary minor is washed out. If Red fails to score and Blue gains control, the referee stops play and the minor proceeds in the usual way.

Multiple infractions make the calculation more complicated. If more than one minor is pending, the NHL rules specify which penalty is affected by the goal and which penalties still must be served. A double minor for certain stick or contact offenses can also be treated differently from a single ordinary minor. Rule 15.3 provides specific treatment for listed double-minor situations, so it is unsafe to assume that every raised-arm call disappears after a goal.

## Delayed call versus delayed offside

A delayed penalty is not the same as delayed offside. Delayed offside concerns whether attacking players have cleared the offensive zone so play can continue legally. A delayed penalty concerns enforcement of an infraction after the non-offending side's possession ends. Both may involve a signal and a whistle later, but the reasons are different.

On a broadcast, look for the referee's raised arm and the attacking team's decision about its goalie. The essential sequence is infraction, signal, continued possession, then either a goal or a whistle when the offending team controls the puck. To determine the penalty afterward, identify whether the original call was a minor, double minor, major or another category rather than treating every delayed call alike.
