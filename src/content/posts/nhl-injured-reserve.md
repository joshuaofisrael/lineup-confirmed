In the NHL, injured reserve (IR) is a roster designation, not a separate team or a guarantee of salary-cap relief. It lets a club remove an unavailable player from its active roster count while preserving the player's contract status. Long-term injured reserve (LTIR) is a different mechanism with additional eligibility conditions and salary-cap implications.

## What ordinary injured reserve changes

Under Article 16 of the NHL/NHLPA collective bargaining agreement, a player placed on the injured reserve list does not count against the club's active roster limit. The Carolina Hurricanes' official transaction primer summarizes the same distinction: IR removes a player from the active roster, but ordinary IR does not by itself increase the club's spending limit.

This is an important difference between roster room and cap room. A team might have room to register a replacement player on its active roster because one player is on IR, yet still need to fit that replacement within its applicable salary-cap accounting. Fans often use 'IR' as shorthand for any absence, but the transaction has a specific administrative effect.

For a simplified example, suppose a club is at the applicable active-roster maximum and moves one eligible player to IR. It opens one active-roster place. That does not mean the original player's salary disappears from the club's cap calculation. Whether another player can be added depends on the club's other roster and financial constraints.

## The seven-day minimum

The CBA specifies that a player placed on the injured reserve list is ineligible to compete in NHL games for at least seven days measured from the relevant date specified in the agreement. Eligibility to be activated begins on the eighth day, provided the required clearance and administrative steps are complete. The clock is not simply a promise that the player will return exactly one week later.


The minimum matters because a club cannot use ordinary IR as an unrestricted same-day roster shuffle. A player can be out longer than the minimum, but the designation has a genuine eligibility consequence while it remains in force.

## How LTIR differs

LTIR has a separate purpose. The Hurricanes' transaction primer describes the familiar threshold of at least 10 games and 24 days of unavailability for LTIR eligibility, alongside the requirement that the player be on IR. It also distinguishes the cap consequence: LTIR can increase a team's permitted spending capacity under the applicable calculation, whereas ordinary IR does not.

The precise cap treatment is more complicated than simply deleting a salary. LTIR relief is a calculation within the collective bargaining framework, and the resulting spending room depends on the team's cap position and other commitments. A club does not receive a blank check equal to every dollar of an unavailable player's contract in every situation.

Imagine two otherwise similar teams each remove one player from the active roster. Team A uses ordinary IR and opens a roster spot without receiving the LTIR spending adjustment. Team B qualifies for LTIR and may use the specific cap relief permitted by the governing rules. Both teams have an inactive player, but their salary-cap options are not necessarily the same.

## How activation works

The CBA sets out a formal process for removing a player from IR. The club must provide the required verification and notifications before the player competes again. Activation must also leave the team in compliance with the applicable roster limit. A team that has filled the open spot while a player was on IR may need a corresponding transaction when that player returns.

For example, a club with a full active roster recalls a replacement while another player is on IR. When the original player becomes eligible to return, the club cannot simply exceed the active-roster maximum indefinitely. It may need to assign, trade or otherwise remove a different player, subject to waiver rules and other restrictions.

That is why a return from IR can coincide with a separate transaction involving another player. The second move is often about roster compliance rather than the eligibility of the returning player itself.

## IR is not the same as a waiver assignment

Waivers and IR solve different problems. Waivers concern the process for making certain players available to other NHL clubs before particular assignments or other transactions. IR concerns an eligible player's inactive roster status. A club cannot assume that placing someone on IR gives it permission to bypass waiver requirements for a different player.

The official transaction primer separates IR, LTIR, loan and recall into different categories for precisely this reason. A loan generally moves a player to another roster, such as an affiliate, and may require waivers depending on the player's status. IR does not mean the player has been loaned to an affiliate.

For readers tracking transactions, the useful questions are: Did the move open an active-roster spot? Did it provide any LTIR cap relief? When does the minimum ineligibility period end? And what corresponding transaction will be necessary to activate the player? Those answers explain the roster implications without requiring speculation about any individual player's medical circumstances.
